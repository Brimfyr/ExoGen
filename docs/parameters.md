# Parameter graph specification

Revision 0.6, 2026-10-11. This document is the contract for the `params` module of `exogen-core`. Every node the user can see or set is listed here with its unit, kind, default or derivation, and the source of the relation. The formula appendix at the end gives the relations by number. Values marked *fit* are calibrated against the reference bodies during the phase that implements the stage; the derivation form is fixed here, the constants are not.

## Implementation status

Phase 1 implements the graph mechanism and the groups star, orbit, body, rotation, interior, atmosphere, volatiles, climate (zero-dimensional subset), cratering (scaling subset), detail, output, seeds and multipliers, plus `tectonics.dichotomy_mode`, `tectonics.dichotomy_amplitude` and `tectonics.initial_crust_thickness`. Phase 2 adds the cratering population nodes (`cratering.loop_threshold`, `cratering.min_diameter`, `cratering.retention_age`, `cratering.empirical_saturation`, `cratering.secondary_fraction`, `cratering.oblique_fraction`), `surface.space_weathering_timescale`, `surface.solidification_time`, the flux history nodes `cratering.early_flux_ratio` and `cratering.early_flux_decay`, and the material controls `materials.bedrock`, `materials.regolith_mix`, `materials.deep_bedrock`, `materials.deep_bedrock_depth` and `materials.colour_space`. The impact flux is written in time since formation, so the crater record begins at the solidification time whatever the body's age; craters carry an impact angle; and each preset carries its own master seed. The executable table is `crates/exogen-core/src/params/registry.rs`; every constant lives in `crates/exogen-core/src/constants.rs` with its source, and constants marked provisional there are calibrated in the phase named. Phase 3 adds the thermal history curves (`interior.heat_flux_history`, `interior.mantle_temperature_history`, `interior.lithosphere_history`, `interior.relief_limit_history`, `interior.contraction_history`, `orbit.eccentricity_history`), `volcanism.rate`, `volcanism.resurfacing_rate` and `volcanism.history`, the contraction nodes `tectonics.contraction_strain` and `tectonics.scarp_total_shortening`, the plume nodes `tectonics.plume_rise_count`, `tectonics.plume_rise_diameter`, `tectonics.plume_rise_height` and `tectonics.rift_extension`, the episodic-lid nodes `tectonics.resurfacing_event_age` and `tectonics.tessera_fraction`, the ice-shell nodes `interior.love_number_h2`, `tectonics.tidal_stress`, `tectonics.ice_crack_density` and `tectonics.nsr_period` (Phase 4), the Io-regime node `tectonics.mountain_count`, the volcanism counts `volcanism.shield_count`, `volcanism.corona_count`, `volcanism.dome_count` and `volcanism.paterae_count`, `volcanism.province_count`, and `materials.lava`. Phase 4 adds the orbital-history nodes `orbit.resonance_age`, `orbit.resonance_eccentricity` and `orbit.resonance_duration` with `interior.tidal_flux_history`, the partial-resurfacing nodes `tectonics.polar_thinning`, `tectonics.ice_resurfaced_fraction`, `tectonics.ice_resurfacing_age` and `tectonics.ice_resurfacing_duration`, `tectonics.chaos_fraction`, `volcanism.cryodome_count`, `volcanism.plume_fallout_rate`, `materials.salt` and `surface.trailing_irradiation`. The fourth revision of Phase 4 derives `atmosphere.bond_albedo` from the surface (F52) with lagged dependencies, marks the composition, inventory and rotation nodes as inputs, so that no reference preset pins a derived node, and adds the focusing-episode nodes `tectonics.focusing_period` and `tectonics.focusing_episode_count` (F53). Phase 4F, the fidelity of the base, adds the sub-grid crater roughness (F54) and the Gaussian pyramid under the renewed terrains, the structure of the focused heating and the local polar folds (F43), variation within units (F55) and Io's accumulated deposits (F56). The remaining tectonics, volcanism, weather, surface-process and material nodes are added by the phases that implement those stages. A test asserts that every derivation reads only the nodes it declares, and `exogen validate` checks the derived values of the sixteen reference presets against measured values. The sixty-ninth milestone adds the thermal-orbital nodes `orbit.resonance_heating`, `orbit.resonance_forced` and `interior.ocean_age` (F138, F140) and the reference preset Mimas.

## Conventions

- Node ids are `group.name`, snake case.
- Units are SI unless the unit column says otherwise. Display units may differ (AU, bar, Gyr, km) and are converted at the UI boundary only.
- **Kinds**: `root` (an input with a default, no derivation), `derived` (computed from upstream nodes; shown dimmed; can be set, which turns it into an override), `expert` (derived or root, hidden unless expert mode is on), `multiplier` (an artistic scale factor, default 1, always visible, never derived), `curve` (a piecewise-linear history over simulated time; its default is a constant or a derived curve), `list` (a user-editable table of records).
- Every derived node records the ids it was derived from, so the UI can show them on hover.
- Setting a derived node pins it. Downstream nodes keep deriving from the pinned value. "Reset to derived" clears the pin. A derived node marked *input* (what the body is made of, its volatile inventory, its rotation state) is a default a preset is expected to set: setting it gives an input, not a pinned outcome, and the reference presets set nothing else. A node may read *lagged* dependencies from the previous recompute pass, so that a feedback (the surface's reflectance sets the temperature that sets the surface) stays acyclic; a recompute repeats its pass until nothing changes, four passes at most.
- Ranges are soft: values outside them raise a plausibility warning, never an error.
- References point to PLAN.md section 12 by author and year.

## Formula appendix

Symbols: G gravitational constant, σ Stefan–Boltzmann, k_B Boltzmann, m_u atomic mass unit. Solar units where stated.

- **F1 Mass–luminosity.** Six-piece fit of Eker et al. 2018, log L = a log M + b (solar units), with (a, b) = (2.028, −0.976) below 0.45 M_sun, (4.572, −0.102) to 0.72, (5.743, −0.007) to 1.05, (4.329, 0.010) to 2.4, (3.967, 0.093) to 7, (2.865, 1.105) to 31; normalised so that one solar mass gives one solar luminosity. This is the reference value at the age fraction of F3.
- **F2 Mass–radius (stars).** R = 0.438 M² + 0.479 M + 0.075 for 0.18 to 1.5 M_sun (Eker et al. 2018); above 1.5 the radius continues as R(1.5) (M / 1.5)^0.555.
- **F3 Luminosity history.** L(t) = L_zams / (1 − 0.286 (t / t_ref)) with t_ref = 0.457 x main-sequence lifetime, which reproduces the Sun at 71% of present luminosity at zero age (Gough 1981 form, rescaled by lifetime).
- **F4 XUV history.** F_xuv ∝ t^−1.23 for t above 0.1 Gyr, saturated before (Ribas et al. 2005), normalised to the present Sun.
- **F5 Kepler.** P = 2π sqrt(a³ / (G (M_primary + m))).
- **F6 Gravity.** g = G m / R².
- **F7 Escape velocity.** v_esc = sqrt(2 G m / R).
- **F8 Bulk composition.** Given (m, R), find the (core, rock, ice) mass fractions whose three-layer model reproduces R, using mass–radius curves for iron, silicate and water-ice end members (Zeng et al. 2016 fits). Two unknowns from one constraint: assume a chondritic core-to-rock ratio of 0.45 unless density exceeds pure rock, in which case ice is zero and the core fraction is solved for. As built, the fractions mix end members by the uncompressed density, the chondritic ratio Earth's 0.48; since F122 a body formed too warm for water ice to condense carries none, its density's deficit below the chondritic mix being iron it lacks.
- **F9 Oblateness.** f = (5/4) Ω² R³ / (G m) x correction for the moment of inertia (Darwin–Radau).
- **F10 Locking timescale.** t_lock = ω a⁶ I Q / (3 G M_p² k₂ R⁵), I = α m R² (Gladman et al. 1996).
- **F11 Rotation regime.** L_Ro = sqrt(sqrt(g H) / (2 Ω / R))... computed as the equatorial Rossby deformation radius L = sqrt(c R / (2 Ω)) with c = sqrt(g H), H the scale height. rapid if L below R/3; slow if L above R; rhines between (Haqq-Misra et al. 2018, simplified).
- **F12 Radiogenic heating.** H(τ) = H_0 Σ_i f_i exp(λ_i τ) for τ = time before present, with H_0 = 5e−12 W per kg of silicate now and (f_i, half-life Gyr): U238 (0.37, 4.47), U235 (0.02, 0.70), Th232 (0.43, 14.0), K40 (0.18, 1.25) (Turcotte and Schubert).
- **F13 Tidal heating.** Ė = (21/2) (k₂/Q) G M_p² R⁵ n e² / a⁶ (Peale, Cassen and Reynolds 1979).
- **F14 Lithosphere thickness.** z = k (T_mantle − T_surface) / q, with k = 3 W/m/K for rock and k = 651 / T W/m/K for ice.
- **F15 Maximum relief.** h_max = c Δσ / (ρ_crust g), c calibrated so Earth gives 9 km, Mars 22 km and Venus 11 km with the strength law of interior.crust_strength. For ice, h_max is the smaller of the strength value and the relaxation-limited value for the body's age.
- **F16 Global contraction.** ΔR = (α_v / 3) ΔT R with α_v = 3e−5 per K and ΔT = 50 K x min(1, (R / R_Mars)²) of mantle cooling plus 250 K times the squared iron index from the uncompressed density (0 at 3.3 g/cm³, 1 at 5.15), accumulating as 1 − exp(−(t − 0.7 Gyr) / 2 Gyr) after a 0.7 Gyr onset during which early heating offsets the cooling. Mercury 5.7 km, Mars 1.7 km, the Moon 0.2 km (Byrne et al. 2014; Watters et al. 2010; Tosi et al. 2013).
- **F17 Magma production.** Rate = max(0, q − q_conductive) x 4π R² / (ρ L_fusion + ρ c_p ΔT) x efficiency, calibrated so Earth gives about 20 km³/yr and Io about 1 cm/yr of resurfacing.
- **F18 Ice shell thickness.** d = k_ice (T_melt − T_surface) / q with k_ice = 651 / T evaluated at the mean shell temperature; T_melt lowered by ammonia if present.
- **F19 Tectonic regime.** ice_shell_ocean if has_subsurface_ocean; ice_shell_rock if composition is ice_dominated or rock_ice without an ocean; mobile_lid if surface liquid water exists and surface_heat_flux is above 0.05 W/m² and radius above 4000 km; episodic_lid if rock with surface_heat_flux above 0.04 and no surface water and radius above 5000 km; stagnant_lid otherwise. Overridable.
- **F20 Scale height.** H = k_B T / (μ m_u g).
- **F21 Greenhouse optical depth.** τ = Σ_species a_s u_s^b_s with u_s the species column mass, a_s and b_s fit to Earth (+33 K), Mars (+5 K), Venus (+500 K) and Titan (+3 K net); water column derived from relative humidity and temperature by Clausius–Clapeyron; N2–N2 and CH4–N2 collision-induced terms scale as column squared. Surface temperature follows T_s⁴ = T_eq⁴ (1 + 3τ/4) in the zero-dimensional estimate.
- **F22 Equilibrium temperature.** T_eq = (L (1 − A_B) / (16 π σ d²))^(1/4); substellar temperature without redistribution is √2 T_eq.
- **F23 Jeans parameter.** λ = G m μ_s m_u / (k_B T R) per species, evaluated at the exobase temperature T_exo = T_s + 700 K x xuv^0.3 x (1 − 0.8 f_CO2) (provisional). A species is flagged below λ = 36, which is the rule of thumb that a gas survives when the escape speed exceeds six times its most probable thermal speed.
- **F24 Crater cut-off.** D_min = c (P/g)^0.6, c such that Earth gives 150 m; this yields about 2.4 km for Venus and 13 m for Mars, to be re-fit against Venus and Titan counts.
- **F25 Vapour pressure.** ln(p/p_t) = (L / R_s)(1/T_t − 1/T) per species about its triple point (T_t, p_t) with latent heat L of vaporisation above T_t and sublimation below.

- **F27 Plate count.** N = round(7 (A / A_Earth)^0.5 (q / 0.087)^0.5), minimum 2.
- **F28 Impact flux.** Relative to the Moon: inner system by heliocentric distance and gravitational focusing per Ivanov 2001 (Mars about 2); outer system per Zahnle et al. 2003 by planet and satellite distance. Replaced by the bombardment of any star (F135): the rocky leftovers by the place against the snow line, the outer system's own comets beyond it.
- **F29 Impact velocity.** v² = v_∞² + v_esc², with v_∞ by location: 0.4 to 1.0 x the local orbital speed for heliocentric impactors, plus parent-planet focusing for moons. - **F30 Simple-to-complex transition.** D_sc = 16.5 km x (1.62 / g) x f_material, f = 1 rock, 0.15 ice.
- **F31 Flux history.** The Neukum chronology N(≥1 km) = 5.44e−14 (e^(6.93 T) − 1) + 8.38e−4 T per km², T in Gyr before present, rewritten in time since formation τ with the present 4.5 Gyr after the Moon formed: rate = c [1 + ρ e^(−τ/τ_d)] with ρ = a b e^(4.5 b) / c ≈ 1.57e4 and τ_d = 1 / b = 0.144 Gyr, zero before the surface solidifies. A body of any age gets its heavy bombardment at formation and the steady flux ever after; the production function is the Neukum 2001 polynomial.
- **F32 Hadley edge.** φ_H = sqrt(5 g H Δ_h / (3 Ω² R²)) in radians, capped at π/2, with H the tropopause height (taken as 2 scale heights, which gives Earth 32°) and Δ_h the fractional equator-to-pole radiative-equilibrium temperature contrast (Held and Hou 1980).
- **F33 Heat transport coefficient.** D = 0.58 W/m²/K x (P / 1 bar)(c_p / c_p,air)(μ_air / μ)(Ω_Earth / Ω)², capped at 100 x the Earth value (Williams and Kasting 1997 scaling as used in exoplanet energy-balance models).

- **F36 Flood volcanism.** Eruptive fraction of F17 = 0.1 / (1 + exp((z_lith − 400 km) / 30 km)) x exp(−(ε / 0.0012)²); eruptions of 100 to 10,000 km³ (log-uniform) from vents weighted by exp(−(crust − min) / 10 km) exp(−(h − median) / 2 km) x (1 + 20 x rise height fraction) x the magmatic province weight (volcanism.province_count); each fills a lake from the nearest low, taking cells in height order where its lava stands at least 50 m deep, a full lake spilling the rest over its lowest pass into the next low (F107), its flows running no further than 1,500 km from the vent, the lava subsiding by 5% of its thickness. The finish's fine pass filled the flood's own footprint level wherever the base still said its lava and no finer pass had written there, reading what stood above the level in those cells as the resampling's ramp toward a higher neighbour, until F120 withdrew that levelling, which cut terraces into real slopes; what stands above the level, a rim, an older floor or a slope, the flood ends against. - **F41 Tidal stress of a thin shell.** The permanent bulge H = h₂ (M_p / M)(R / a)³ R towards the planet; membrane strains ε_θθ = (L P₂'' + H P₂) / R and ε_φφ = (L cot θ P₂' + H P₂) / R about the bulge axis with L = 0.25 H, plane stress with E = 9 GPa and ν = 0.33. The diurnal field at mean anomaly M is the field of a bulge (1 + 3e cos M) times the permanent one about an axis librated by 2e sin M, less the permanent field; non-synchronous rotation adds the field of the axis advanced by 1° less the permanent field. Cracks open perpendicular to the most tensile principal stress where it exceeds 40 kPa.
- **F42 Lineae.** The population carries π density / 2 of crack length per unit area, drawn per time bin within the retention age, lengths log-uniform 0.064 to 0.96 radii (Europa 100 to 1,500 km); a crack nucleates in proportion to the tension above the threshold, runs along the field of its time where the slow field dominates, and where the diurnal tension is more than 60% of the whole it propagates at 0.8 m/s only while the tension lasts and turns with the field through the orbit, arcing between cusps; 15% of the ridges pull apart into bands 0.0064 to 0.019 radii wide (Europa 10 to 30 km), each a lens whose width goes as (4 s (1 − s))^0.35 along its length, lineated inside at 1.5 km (or three cells) by 40 m. Under a plume the fissures of the thinned shell are vents: troughs 450 m deep and 6 km wide with flanks 40% of the depth. The ocean's salts in a crack fade under frost over 0.03 Gyr. Cracks answer one another (Pollard and Aydin 1988): 0.35 of new cracks start at the tip of an older one, where its stress concentrates, and run one way from it at the field's heading of their own time, so that sets branch and splay; a propagating crack within half a shell thickness of an open fissure (a vent) feels its stress shadow and turns toward meeting it at a right angle, stopping within half a width of it as at a free surface, while healed ridges and bands are crossed. Every crack's relief and width rise and fall along it by 0.35 rms over 15 km segments, so ridges and troughs pinch and swell rather than run uniform.
- **F43 Focused heating.** The shell under one pole is thinner than the mean by the polar thinning times a cosine taper that reaches the mean 70° from the pole, the edge wobbling by 15% with a two-cycle field, and the thinning structured across the zone by the shell's convection, up and down by 0.3 in cells two shell thicknesses wide, so that the zone's outline, its depression and its renewed terrain are lobate rather than circular; the replayed craters' survival and the crack density follow the local thickness (the density as 1 / d); the loop's relaxation keeps the mean shell's rates, a soft zone given its own band factors beside a stiff one sinking many times its offset. The renewed terrain, once the thinning passes 0.3, is the taper's core (above 0.75) and the ground round the fissures that opened where the taper stands above a half: within 30 km of them in the core, narrowing to nothing where the taper falls to a half, so that it follows the fissures out as tongues and its outline is theirs; a fissure leaving the core meets the thicker, stiffer shell and refracts - **F44 Grooved terrain.** Lanes of new ice are swaths about centre lines traced along the tidal field at their time where the shell cracks and the tension at their start opens it, so that they curve with the field as Enceladus's sulci do, and along great circles where nothing steers them, as Ganymede's; widths log-uniform 4 to 24 thicknesses of the elastic lid where and when the lane opens (F61; a lane is a swath, a rift zone a few to a few tens of times the layer that breaks, which does not scale with the body: 44 to 266 km on Ganymede's 11 km lithosphere of the episode), lengths 6 to 40 widths, log-uniform, at most 1.5 rad (Ganymede's lanes hundreds to a couple of thousand kilometres) their edges and ends as F61 has them, their midpoints gathered by rejection into belts of extension drawn by a 1.2-cycle field squared; 0.85 of them are laid beside one of the 24 lanes laid lately, in its generation (its time within 5% of the episode), turned by up to 3°, their centre lines 0.4 to 0.85 of the sum of their half widths apart so that the two always overlap and a set is one swath and between half and all of its length, so that they come in parallel sets; the rest run along the strike of a smooth field of extension (1.2 cells per radian about the body, read as an angle from east) that drifts by a cell through the episode, so that generations cross-cut instead of every lane taking a heading of its own; drawn until they cover the resurfaced fraction, at most 12,000; a fifth of them are smooth bright terrain. The amount comes from the peak tidal flux of the body's history (the pulse of a resonance episode, F50, or the steady flux), and each lane forms at its own time, uniform over tectonics.ice_resurfacing_duration about tectonics.ice_resurfacing_age, the spread and the mean of the time the flux spent above the floor; at that time the relief gives way, by the lane's cover of each cell (F60), to the relief smoothed over 100 km (or R / 20), and drops 200 m, the drop spread over the lithosphere's flexural parameter at the time ((4 D / rho g)^1/4, about 90 km on Ganymede today and 55 km during its episode, 130 km on Enceladus) so that a lane narrower than it sinks little and no lane ends on a step, the surface age resets and the unit becomes grooved terrain, a younger lane overprinting an older, clean ice whatever the regolith around it; the finish redraws the lanes at its own resolution, completes the renewal out to their resolved edges (F60) and lays their fabric (F61). Craters formed since cut the grooves; craters formed before are gone.
- **F49 Thermal segregation on slopes.** A slope rising towards the pole faces the equator and a slope rising towards the equator faces the pole, so each cell of an icy surface sits at the mean temperature of the latitude its slope behaves as, |lat| − atan(poleward slope), for its frost and its lag; frost lies on pole-facing walls and leaves equator-facing ones, and the cap's edge follows the ground.
- **F45 Chaos** (its patches replaced by F80's generations). Patches of diameter log-uniform 5 to 300 km, their number the chaos fraction of the surface over the mean patch area, drawn per time bin within the retention age at places accepted by a two-cycle heat field whose troughs take half as many as its crests; inside an outline wobbling by 35% in about six waves round the patch the matrix drops 150 m and breaks into hummocks of 60 m at 1.5 km around rafts of the old surface (45% of the patch at a 15 km scale) that keep what was on them; the matrix takes the surface age, the chaos unit and the ocean's salts, which fade like a crack's.
- **F46 Cryovolcanism.** On a shell over rock 0.7 of the ice magma budget extrudes as domes, per time bin while the budget allows, of volumes log-uniform 1e-6 to 3e-5 R³, each a bell of height 0.24 of its diameter (V = (2π/3) x 0.24 r³) placed at random, relaxing afterwards like any load of its wavelength by F39; its substrate is half clean ice and carries salts that darken over 0.15 Gyr. A crater deeper than 3 km brings brines up: salts in patches at 4 km over the inner 35% of its floor, darkening over 0.15 Gyr.
- **F47 Plume fallout.** A polar plume lays fresh ice down at the rate volcanism.plume_fallout_rate at its pole, falling off as exp(−angle / 25°) to 1% of it far away; it buries a millimetre of lag or salt in that depth over the rate, which caps the exposure the lag maturity sees and the time the salts show, and keeps a full frost cover wherever it exceeds 1e3 m/Gyr.
- **F48 Hemispheric asymmetry.** The sub-parent point lies on +x and the orbit runs prograde about +z, so a locked moon's trailing apex faces +y; the parent's plasma darkens and reddens the ice there by the tint (0.76, 0.69, 0.62) at the apex, falling off as the squared cosine of the angle from it, scaled by surface.trailing_irradiation.
- **F50 Resonance episode.** An orbital resonance holds the eccentricity up for a while: e(t) is the present value with a pulse to orbit.resonance_eccentricity centred orbit.resonance_age before the present and orbit.resonance_duration wide at its base, rising over the first fifth of the episode after the capture, holding at the forced value, and falling over the last fifth after the escape (Showman and Malhotra 1997: the Galilean moons' passage into the Laplace resonance pumped Ganymede's eccentricity to 0.01 to 0.07 for 10 Myr to 1 Gyr; Bland, Showman and Tobie 2009 on the heating pulse that followed). The tidal flux follows e² (F13), so the pulse is a pulse of heating in every history downstream: the shell thins with it and the relaxation of the craters of the time follows (F39), and the lanes of grooved terrain take their amount from its peak and their times from its spread (F44; since F140 their amount from the heat carried over the episode). Ganymede's preset carries the episode, 0.008 for a gigayear two gigayears ago, which thins its shell to about 40 km, enough to relax the craters over 150 km to palimpsests and not the 70 km ones (a stronger pulse flattens those too, which the dark terrain does not show), and pins nothing.
- **F51 Ejecta cover and mixed exposure.** A blanket's deposit thins as r⁻³ (McGetchin et al. 1973) and covers the ground it falls on by 1 − 2^−(edge / r)³: complete out to 1.3 radii (a raw cover above 0.92 counts as complete, the cover being scaled by it), half at the continuous edge (two radii, wandering by 15% in eleven to sixty-one lobes round the crater, the lobes developing outward, a lobe narrower than four cells along the edge left undrawn since at the grid's scale it would be a tooth from a continuous rim zone), a fifth at three radii, tapering to nothing over the outer quarter of the footprint, where the deposit is too thin to leave a record. The covered cell keeps one age, the one whose exposure gives the mixture's maturity under a law that saturates on the surface's maturity timescale τ: a = t_event + τ ln(f + (1 − f) e^−(t_event − a_old)/τ), exact for an exponential law at every later time; τ is surface.space_weathering_timescale on rock and, on ice, the exposure at which the lag at the body's mean temperature reaches 1 − 1/e. Where the cover is under a half the ground keeps its unit, its melt and its salts or lava. The ray field brightens the blanket by the same cover and runs its rays out from the rim through it, so a fresh crater's disc grades into its rays and an old crater's into the ground, with no circle at either.
- **F52 The surface's own albedo.** An airless body (under 10 Pa) has the Bond albedo its surface gives it: the luminance of the regolith mixture at maturity, raised to the fresh luminance over the share of the body a plume's fallout keeps under frost (the frost cover of F47 integrated over the sphere). The surface depends on the temperature the albedo sets, through the shell, the regime and the materials, so the albedo reads the regolith mixture and the fallout rate as lagged dependencies and the graph settles in a pass or two. Against the measured Bond albedos: Moon 0.14 (0.11), Mercury 0.085 (0.088), Ceres 0.086 (0.09), Europa 0.66 (0.67), Enceladus 0.83 (0.81), Triton 0.66 (0.76), Ganymede 0.37 (0.43, the bright lanes not counted), Io 0.37 (0.52), Callisto 0.13 (0.22, its bright craters not counted); Pluto 0.25 (0.5) until its nitrogen and methane ices are among the materials. Under an atmosphere clouds, haze and dust set the planetary albedo, which the climate stage will compute; until then the pressure class stands in. No preset pins the albedo.
- **F53 Episodes of focusing.** The focused heating comes and goes (O'Neill and Nimmo 2010: the shell overturns every 0.1 to 1 Gyr, each overturn active about 10 Myr): episodes recur every tectonics.focusing_period, forty conductive times of the shell (a fit in their range, so that Enceladus's come 0.52 Gyr apart; ten while its shell stood at 44 km, refitted when the porous core of F68 brought it to its measured 22 km), each 10 Myr long. Each starts where the overturn happens to (a direction from the tectonic seed), and the body reorients to put it at the pole (Nimmo and Pappalardo 2006), so in the present frame the terrain it renewed lies about that direction with its fissures turned by the same rotation. Through an episode the zone floats at its isostatic depression and renews itself every step as the present one does, its fissures opening in the polar field of its time, a share of all the thinned zone's cracks falling to each episode alike; when it ends the shell floats back up and the terrain takes craters from then on. An episode renews its terrain once in the loop, at the step its end falls in, whatever the step's length (F60). The finish redraws each fossil terrain's outline from its carried fissures and lays its folds and margin at its time, in sequence with everything else; its fissures vented until it ended, and stand on the last surface it renewed. Enceladus carries eight of them, which with the present terrain and the lanes renew 0.40 of the surface (the sidecar's renewed_fraction); the rest stays cratered plains, 60% against the 43% Kirchoff and Schenk 2009 mapped, each past terrain being the size of the present one.
- **F54 Sub-grid crater roughness.** The craters smaller than the finish stamps (below cratering.min_diameter or two cells) are a population too, and each cell's surface age says how many it has taken: at the smallest resolved diameter D the production function and the flux history give the density since the surface formed, against the equilibrium 0.05 of geometric saturation, and the share reached, s = 1 − e^−(N / N_eq). Their relief goes under everything as a self-affine field with octaves from D down to two cells, each 2^−H as strong (H 0.8, the Hurst exponent of cratered terrain, Rosenburg et al. 2011), scaled so that a saturated surface's rms is 0.35 of the depth of a crater of diameter D, times √s. An old surface is rough at every scale, a renewed one smooth, and their contact is a gradient of roughness; the strength follows the surface age smoothed over two cells (twelve passes of the one-cell kernel), so the edge of a young deposit on old ground is a short ramp and never a staircase of cells. The renewed terrains' floors come from a Gaussian pyramid (blur, halve, expand level by level, blur) that leaves no block of the loop's cells, and a renewed cell always drops the finest band.
- **F55 Variation within units.** A body's lavas drift in composition over its eruptive history: a flow unit's reflectance departs from its material's by up to 18%, by a smooth function of the eruption's time with a period of 1 Gyr, which the eruption writes into the surface's composition layer (the loop's and the finish's alike), so units close in time are alike, units an age apart differ, and a crater's ejecta of the same basalt keeps its unit's tone; no mixed age is ever read as a tone (the maria's titanium, albedos 0.06 to 0.11). A surface age itself resamples by its nearest coarse cell, never a blend of two events' times. Steep ground sheds its regolith: from 20°, the regolith's stable slope, the maturity of every airless surface falls, by 0.3 of the way at the angle of repose, 30°, from the ground's own slope (optical maturity maps: the walls of young craters bright, those of old craters near the stable slope only a little less mature than their surroundings), on rock and ice alike. A lane of renewed ice is clean by a share drawn uniformly in 0.55 to 1, so the lanes differ in brightness as Ganymede's do.
- **F56 Io's flows accumulate.** Every patera carries three generations of flow fields, the older with lobes from 150 years to 300,000 years old, six to fourteen lobes each traced over the ground (F59), so the plains are a patchwork of half-buried flows. A flow's coverage varies by 0.4 over 15 km, the overlapping flow units within a lobe. Until F84 a flow faded on a fixed 20,000 years and each patera carried three generations of plume rings faded by their ages; since F84 a flow is coated by what falls on it and the plumes are an eruption history.
- **F59 Flows follow the ground.** A lobe of a flow field is a path traced from the patera's rim at a random azimuth, 0.3 to 1 of the field's reach long: at each step of 2 km or a 64th of its length the heading turns toward the fall line of the regional ground (the base smoothed over 40 km, since a front rides over bumps shorter than its width on its own pile) by 2 tanh(slope / 0.005), sideways along a contour and onward down a slope but never back on itself, and wanders by up to 1 rad over 40 km of path, so a flow runs downhill where the ground slopes and meanders where it is flat; it stops where a mountain stands or where going on would climb more than 150 m above the lowest ground it has reached, as a flow ponds in a basin and crosses a bump no higher than its own pile. The lobe is a tube about that line, its half width 0.05 to 0.15 of the reach (at least 3 km), 0.4 of that at the vent widening to the full at the toe and wandering by 0.25, its margin lobate by 0.3 at the width's scale and soft over 0.15 of the width or a cell and a half; the strongest lobe's coverage and the freshest lava win where lobes cross. The finish lays the regime's surface afresh before it stamps anything, as every loop step does, so the loop's coarse stamps leave no halo of unit or coverage round a feature. A mountain block ends in a fault scarp on its upthrown side (its rim falling as 1 − x¹²) and slopes under the plains on the downthrown (1 − x²), so it is no longer an oval with a steep edge all round (a thrust block since F87). Since F85 a path starts at any point along a heading and turns toward the fall line at a rate per distance, 2 tanh(slope / 0.005) over 20 km of path at full slope (per step before, so that short lobes swung onto the regional slope within a few steps).
- **F57 The tiger-stripe cascade.** Under a plume the fissures of the thinned zone form in a cascade (Hemingway, Rudolph and Manga 2020): the first opens through the thinnest shell, within half a spacing of the pole, and runs along the tidal field while the thin shell lets it, refracting round the margin as a polar crack does; the ice its eruptions pile along its flanks (two Gaussian ridges, each 0.4 of the trough's depth over an eighth of the vent's width) loads a plate broken at the fissure, whose surface tension peaks a quarter flexural parameter away, x = α π / 4 with α⁴ = 4 D / (ρ g) and D = E te³ / (12 (1 − ν²)), the moment there 0.3225 V α and the stress 6 M / te²; the next fissure opens there on either side, and so on outward while the site is still in the thinned core (profile above 0.75) and the bending and the tidal tension together exceed the strength, up to eight a side. The elastic thickness at a site is the body's (interior.elastic_thickness) times the shell's local thinning factor, so the spacing follows from the parameters rather than from the 35 km of the real set (which the paper reproduces with 5.2 km of elastic ice). A fissure wanders by 0.3 rad over 50 km, more than a lesser crack, and ends in a fork: from each tip a branch leaves at 60° and bends back to the field over 15 km, running a quarter of the fissure's length at 0.6 of its width, the hooked, bifurcating terminations of the real stripes. The zone's other cracks are its lesser fractures, drawn as before but no longer vents. Each past episode's cascade is drawn in the pole's frame and carried to its fossil terrain.
- **F58 Plasma under the fallout.** The trailing hemisphere's plasma darkening (F48) processes the optical surface, the top millimetre, in 10⁴ years in a Jupiter-class magnetosphere and proportionally longer in a weaker one (Cooper et al. 2001: micrometres on Europa in about ten years, centimetres in under a million); where a plume's fallout buries that millimetre first, the darkening shows by t_bury / (t_bury + t_dark), so Enceladus's trailing side stays as bright as the rest under its frost while Europa's bullseye keeps its full tint.
- **F60 Renewed terrains keep their history.** A lane, a polar terrain or a past episode's terrain renews the ground once, at its time, and everything since stands on what it left. In the loop a terrain's renewal is a weight on each cell. For a polar terrain: 1 inside and a 15 km ramp across its edge (F43); the ground beyond relaxes under the episode's thinned shell instead (F66). For a lane: its cover, a ramp across its border zone (F61). A cell gives up its relief shorter than the renewal length (a polar terrain's is the larger of the lanes' smoothing length and 0.2 R; a lane's its smoothing length; since F141 a polar terrain's is its zone's radius and a lane's its own width, and the cell keeps the base relief below that length, toned down below the terrain's fabric) by its weight and keeps the rest with its neighbours, so a renewed cell never stands apart from them. A past episode fires once, at the step its end falls in (a 10 Myr episode is far shorter than a step, and a rule that waits for a step to end inside it fires one time in five); the present terrain renews at every step while its episode lasts and alone floats at its depression. The finish lays no floor of its own on a polar terrain: the loop's relief stands, craters since included. It draws each outline at its resolution (a polar terrain's as the signed great-circle distance to its edge), composes the terrains in the order they formed (each one's clean ice laid over what was there by its cover; the latest to take more than half a cell holds its age and unit), and at each terrain's turn in the sequence takes the fine relief stamped before it by the same weight, back to the relief the loop left: a small crater inside is gone, one across the edge is softened by degrees and not cut, and the unresolved craters' roughness (F54) is taken in the same measure. A crater older than a terrain is therefore stamped whole in the finish and taken by the terrain after; the loop, which lays a step's lanes before the step's craters, still keeps such a crater off them. The lanes' renewal is completed on the resolved grid before anything is stamped: where the lane's resolved cover c exceeds what the loop renewed of the cell q (the loop's weight, resampled and smoothed over its cell), the relief shorter than the smoothing length gives way by (c - q) / max(1 - q, 0.25) to the mean of the ground about it (in full where the loop left more than a quarter of the relief; where it left less, the little that remains is not worth a seam along the edge of a younger crater's ground), the craters younger than the lane left out of the mean and left standing. No floor is carried from a lane's time to the finish: a floor recorded at one time and laid at another put a notch hundreds of metres deep along every lane margin whose ground had moved between.
- **F61 A lane is a rift.** One mechanism on every body: a lane is the rift a lid of thickness H makes under extension, and its width, its grooves, its border and its damage zone all scale with H, the elastic lid where and when the lane opens: the lid of the time, thinner by the shell's own thinning where a focusing episode, a past one or the present, has the shell thinned (11 km on Ganymede; 10 to 13 km where Enceladus's lanes open in the thinned zones of its episodes, 23 km outside them). Width: 4 to 24 H (F44). A rift is long: a lane that could not be six times as long as it is wide within the longest a lane may be, 1.5 rad, is not a lane and is not formed, so that no lane is wider than a quarter of the radius, and a draw on a lid too thick for that is a rift that never opened. A lane's time, the place it opens and its width in lid thicknesses are drawn together, and drawn again, up to 4,000 times, until the lid there and then lets a lane that wide be one; the first draw uses the streams it always did, so a body whose lid lets every lane form is not changed by the rule (Ganymede's maps were identical under it). A lane does not open in ground a focusing episode is renewing about it, which keeps no trace. On Enceladus the lid of the time lets no lane form, 4 H being 91 km against the 63 km a lane may be: its lanes open only in the zones its episodes thinned, during those episodes, as sets of belts 44 to 61 km wide (4.3 to 5.9 lid thicknesses) along the margins of its old terrains, and the later terrains overprint the older sets. A body between gets both kinds (principle 15): an Enceladus heated less (0.8 of its eccentricity) makes lanes only in its episodes, and made a fifth larger makes its first few on the lid of the time, half again as large some two dozen there; a fully heated one half again as large makes 86 on the lid of the time and 5 in its episodes (with F75's tides; the counts before were a fifth larger, a few, and half again as large, four fifths and the rest in its episodes). Grooves: the necking of the lid, 3.5 thicknesses of the layer that necks (Fletcher and Hallet 1983 find 2.5 to 6 for the Basin and Range; Dombard and McKinnon 2001 apply the instability to Ganymede's ice), that layer in a rift, hot as a rift is, being 0.18 H: the product, 0.63 H, is one calibration, on Ganymede's grooves at 7 km, which puts Enceladus's at 6.4 to 8.2 km on the thinned lid its lanes break. Their relief is 0.036 of their spacing (250 m at 7 km, Squyres 1981; Enceladus's ridged plains, 100 to 400 m at 3 to 4 km, fall in the same ratio, Bland et al. 2007), whatever spacing a grid draws them at, and no grid draws them closer than three cells. On the necks rides the fine set, the tilted fault blocks (Pappalardo et al. 1998 and Patel et al. 1999: necks about 8 km apart and fault blocks 1 to 2 km apart on Ganymede): six to a groove spacing, 0.4 of the relief, each a long back slope and a short scarp facing the lane's axis, with a wander and segments of their own, drawn where they span three cells (1.1 to 1.4 km on Enceladus, at the edge of what its 387 m cells draw; Ganymede's 1.2 km only on a grid finer than 400 m). Every length of the fabric is in groove spacings: sets change over 40, with the spacing varying by 0.3 and the strength from 0.5 to 1; the grooves are a wave across the lane that sways by 6 rad of phase over 15 spacings and wanders by 2 rad over 3.5, so that they fan and bend; each groove lives in segments about 9 spacings long, with its own run of presence along itself, so that a groove dies out while its neighbours carry on; under them a tectonic roughness of 0.3 of the relief, self-affine from 3 spacings down to the grid. A smooth lane (a fifth of them) carries the same fabric at 0.3 of the relief (Ganymede's smooth bright terrain is finely grooved at Galileo's resolution; Pappalardo et al. 2004). The border: a lane meets the old ground across a zone one H wide, its bounding faults cutting the whole lid, a physical width, the same fault on any grid; a grid's own ramp of 2.5 cells is only the floor where it cannot draw the zone narrower. The edges are the bounding faults' traces: each wanders by 0.08 of the half width rms, self-affine with a Hurst exponent of 0.8 below 6 widths, and is made of segments 12 spacings long; where one relays to the next the trace steps by 0.5 to 1.5 spacings across a relay ramp 3 spacings long, so that it runs straight and bends, the offsets reverting to the mean trace. The fault throws 1.2 times the grooves' relief where its segments stand (0.6 of the trace on average): the lane's side down, decaying over a spacing, and the flank up, decaying over two. Beyond it the damage zone reaches 3 spacings: the grooves go on as open fractures 0.6 of the relief deep, each in fewer and shorter segments the farther out, so that the lane's strain thins out through discrete breaks instead of fading. The ends: every end stops at a cut (since F142 only an end inside an older lane; the rest taper). Where a lane enters an older lane that crosses it at more than 30°, the older lane takes up its extension with probability 0.7, once the lane has run 1.5 widths: the cut runs along the older lane's strike, a cell and a quarter of that lane's half width inside its edge. A lane of a set ends on its parent's cut, so a set ends on one front. An end that has run its length looks on to 3 times it (no farther than the longest a lane may be) for either; finding none, it dies out in the old ground at a fault of its own across the lane, 55° to 90° to it as transfer faults are, whose trace wanders as the edges do and carries the same throw and damage zone. On Ganymede two fifths of the ends stop against an older lane and the rest share some 250 fault fronts, a set to a front; running on to a junction makes its lanes 1,800 km long at the median and 4,500 km at the longest, where its named sulci run from Tiamat's 1,330 km to Mysia's 4,221 km. The tone follows the structure: a lane's ice is clean by 0.8 to 1 (its age does the rest through the lag, F40), sets differ by up to 0.15, troughs gather the dark lag and are darker by up to 0.3 (Prockter et al. 1998), and a fracture's walls show ice 0.6 as clean as the lane's in the old ground. At a lane's turn in the finish the fine relief inside its border gives way by its cover to its floor with its own grooves, so a younger lane cuts an older one's grooves and never lies under them. What only one body reaches: no number in this entry is set for one body. The bound of a quarter of the radius turns lanes away on Enceladus alone among the reference bodies, which is the statement that its lid is thick against its size. A switch does remain upstream, F44's rule that a shell thinner than 0.025 R (0.02 until F75) makes no lanes at all: an Enceladus twice as large loses its lanes in one step there, and that rule is on the list for the audit against principle 15.
- **F62 A crack is a chain of segments.** A ridge or a cycloid is drawn as segments about 12 widths long, their ends jittered by 0.3 of a segment. Each opens as an ellipse along its length, as a crack in an elastic plate does (Pollard and Segall 1987), so that its relief falls to 0.3 where one segment hands over to the next; each stands aside from the last by 0.25 to 0.75 of the width, to the one side all along a crack, en echelon; and the whole crack closes toward its own tips as the fourth root of the same ellipse. A band and a vent are single openings and keep their own profiles. A crack that opens under loose cover shows as pits instead (F64).
- **F63 A rift works in pulses, and its grooves differ in depth.** The first pulse lays a set of grooves along the whole lane (F61). Each of two later pulses reworks part of it with a set of its own: struck along the field the lane follows as that field stands later in the lane's working life (the strike field of extension as it drifts, or the tidal field as it turns; the life is a set's spread of the episode, 5% of it, or what is left of the focusing episode that thinned the lid, if less), which turns Ganymede's later sets by 8 degrees at the median and more than 21 for one in ten, and leaves Enceladus's within half a degree of the first; spaced, swayed and segmented on its own; and standing in patches about 20 groove spacings across, where a field of its own is high, two fifths of the lane, the rest keeping what it had. Where a later groove stands, across its own width and along its own segment, it cuts what stood there before, so the sets overlap groove by groove and never add as two waves would. The fine set follows whichever set stands at a cell. A groove's depth has a heavy tail: fault throws along a line across an array follow a power law with a cumulative exponent of 0.5 to 1 (Marrett and Allmendinger 1992), a necked lid spaces and sizes its grooves more evenly than faults at random, and the share of grooves more than x times as deep as the middling one is taken to fall as x to the power -2, a choice between the two and not a measurement, bounded softly at 2.5 times the middling depth, about the deepest measured (700 m crest to trough against a middling 350 m on Ganymede; Squyres 1981). Half the grooves are shallower than the middling one by up to three tenths and one in eight is more than 1.7 times as deep; what a groove has beyond the middling depth goes into its trough, a master trough, and not onto its crest. The folds of a polar terrain take the same tail to the power 0.58, a kernel being a packet of about three folds whose mean varies less than one fold does; and where a field three kernel cells across stands high a kernel may belong to an earlier pulse, and then keeps the strike of the stress field as it stood then, turned by up to 25 degrees by a field that stands for the unresolved turn of the stress through the episode as the body reorients, at 0.7 of the relief and in phase with its own neighbours.
- **F64 Loose cover and pit chains.** The plumes' fallout (F47) is a loose cover: each episode of focusing (F53) had a plume at its centre while it lasted, venting as the present one does, and the cover on a surface is the sum of every plume's fallout there since the surface formed. On Enceladus that is 400 to 1,900 m on ground as old as the body, the same order as the 250 to 700 m its pits imply and two to three times over (Martin et al. 2017), nine plumes having run where the real body's history is not known. An open fracture under loose cover drains the cover into itself and shows at the surface as a row of pits (Wyrick et al. 2004 for Mars). A fracture drains no more than it is deep: a fracture of a lane's damage zone its own depth (F61), a crack the depth to which its tension holds it open against the weight of the ice, the stress that cracks the shell over the ice's density and the gravity, 380 m on Enceladus. A pit is a cone at the cover's angle of repose, 2.9 drained thicknesses across (2 / tan 35 degrees) and as deep as what drained, so 1.1 km across and 380 m deep on Enceladus's old plains (the real plains' 250 to 700 m of cover would make pits 0.7 to 2 km across by the same rule); pits stand one every pit and a half along the fracture, each up to 0.3 of a step off its place and 0.7 to 1.3 of the size, and only where the fracture's own segment stands, so the chain breaks as the fracture does; a grid draws them where they span a cell and a half. In a lane's damage zone the cover fills a fracture by its depth over the fracture's, the open trough showing by what is left and the pits standing for what is filled. A ridge or a cycloid that opened under cover deeper than its own relief built no ridges at the surface and shows as pits alone, with no salts and no unit of its own; one that opened on bare ground keeps its ridges under whatever fell since. So the same crack runs as a ridge across a young terrain and breaks into pits where it enters the old snow-covered plains. A body with no plume has no loose cover any grid could draw pits in.
- **F65 Deep folds grow and link.** A renewed plain's fold fabric (F43) rides on a first-order train of deep folds three times its wavelength (7.5 km at the edge of Enceladus's terrain's core, going with the lid as the fabric's spacing does; the highest known ridge on Enceladus, at Samarkand Sulcus, is 10 km wide, Giese et al. 2017, its ridge-and-trough terrain is spaced 3 to 4 km, Bland et al. 2007, and its funiscular folds 1.1 km, Barr and Preuss 2010: the order is a choice in that range). Its hinges nucleate one to a wavelength, on a jittered lattice wherever the fabric stands, each with a depth of its own from the heavy tail of F63; the fabric's packets keep the body of their tail. A hinge comes up when its fold has amplified enough, growing exponentially from its initial depth, so the deeper come up sooner: 1.2 units of the growth's time for each factor e by which its depth falls short of the deepest a lid holds (2.5 times the middling fold). One that comes up within 0.8 of a wavelength of a fold already grown is part of it. It grows from its nucleus both ways along the folds' direction (the fissures' direction as the fabric reads it), a quarter wavelength at a step, at a rate that goes with its depth, every hinge at once in the order each step is reached, turning no more than 15 degrees in a step and deflected by up to 12 degrees, in patches four wavelengths across, by the lid's unresolved heterogeneity. A tip that comes within 0.55 of a wavelength of another fold's hinge links with it unless it is running away from it (Grasemann and Schmalholz 2012: fold segments link when their hinges lie slightly more than half a wavelength apart or less, and overlap en echelon when farther apart). Where the two run within 50 degrees of each other it bends into the other where its own heading meets it, or, running alongside, no more steeply than 10 degrees, along a cubic that leaves the tip along its heading and arrives along the other, and beyond the junction the two are one fold: a Y where the other runs on; where the other ends there, into its end, which then neither plunges nor, if it was coming the other way, grows on. A tip of the other coming the other way past the junction stops too. Across a wider angle the tip runs straight on into the fold ahead. A tip passes a neighbour it is running away from, one that ends short of where it would join it or lies behind it, and one it could join only across a third fold. No hinge crosses another: a growth step that meets another fold ends there. A tip stops free where the fabric's strength (the terrain's, times the fade away from the fissures) falls under a quarter, half a wavelength short of one of its terrain's own vents, or when the shortening ends: a fold of twice the middling depth, unhindered, reaches about four and a half wavelengths each way, nine in all, as Samarkand's ridge is ten times as long as it is wide (100 km by 10 km and 1.75 km high, Giese et al. 2017). A hinge nucleates no nearer its terrain's vents than a wavelength; a younger crack across the terrain cuts its folds and does not bound them. So the deep hinges grow long first and the shallow ones fill the gaps and link into them: where the train converges its hinges merge, and where they run apart they end en echelon. A fold stands highest where it nucleated and lower, by up to 0.3, where it reached last, so that a linkage leaves a saddle as linked folds do; a free end plunges over a wavelength. Its profile is a narrow crest, a Ricker profile an eighth of the wavelength wide with a trough each side that takes up what the crest stands above, between the train's broad troughs, as the folds of a thin-skinned belt are (ejective folds). One of the middling depth is as steep as the fabric's folds, 430 m high on Enceladus, a deeper one taller in proportion, the deepest about a kilometre. The folds are one surface: the profile is taken about the distance to the nearest hinge, softened over a quarter of a crest's width where two are nearly as near, with the hinges' heights and wavelengths in the same measure, so that two hinges merge where they meet, the one that runs on unbroken, and a trough between two folds is shared. The fabric's folds give way to a deep fold by a half where it stands, the rest riding on its limbs. A past episode's deep folds are spread and lowered by the gardening since over their crests' width.
- **F66 The ground beside a renewed terrain relaxes under the thinned shell.** A renewed terrain takes the old relief inside its outline and across the ramp at its edge (F60), and no farther: no halo is drawn from the outline. Beside it the old ground relaxes by the law of F39 under the thinner shell of the episode that renewed the terrain, for as long as the episode lasted, the thinning read from the episode's own map: in the loop each wavelength band shorter than the renewal length keeps its survival under the episode's shell over its survival under the shell as it was; in the finish each replayed crater relaxes likewise under every past episode it lived through. On Enceladus, whose 44 km shell thinned to 0.45 of itself still keeps a 25 km crater whole through a 10 Myr episode and loses a 100 km load, the cratered plains run on crisp to the margin's ramp; under a thinner shell, as on an Enceladus half again as large, the craters beside a terrain relax by size, the largest first, as Enceladus's relaxed craters follow its heat flow (Bland et al. 2012). The halo it replaces took up to half of every crater's relief out to one mean shell thickness from the outline, whatever its size, which the relaxation law does not support on Enceladus, and copied the outline, tongues and all, into the old ground as a smooth ramp.
- **F67 A complex crater's wall is made by its collapse.** The transient cavity's rim slumps inward on inward-facing normal faults in rings of blocks (Settle and Head 1979): terrace after terrace from the rim inward, the outermost 0.09 D^0.87 km wide on the Moon (Pearce and Melosh 1986, a fit to lunar craters that a slump model with a constant yield strength reproduces), each inward three quarters of the one outside it, the widths growing outward toward the rim as theirs do, until one would be narrower than 0.35 of the outermost or the terraces would fill more than 0.85 of the wall; below them the wall is a plain slope of debris to the floor. A terrace's width depends on the crater's size and on the strength length Y / (rho g) of the collapsing target, so it goes as that length to the power the fit leaves, 0.13; the simple-to-complex transition diameter measures the same length, so a body's terraces scale with its transition against the Moon's, with no constant of their own: Mercury's come out a tenth narrower than the Moon's at a given size, as Leith and McKinnon (1991) measured them narrower, Ganymede's a fifth narrower and Enceladus's a tenth wider. A terrace is a ring of slump blocks, each about five of its widths long and longer or shorter by up to a factor of two, its scarp standing off its neighbours' by up to 0.35 of the width as the throw of the faults varies around a crater, and bowed out toward the rim by a quarter of the width in its middle as a slump's headscarp is; a fifth of the blocks slumped no terrace. A bench keeps 0.35 of the wall's slope; its scarp is a fifth of its width wide, half as wide again or half as narrow from block to block, wanders along its trace by 0.15 of the width at the scale of a few widths, and widens with the crater's degradation; a slumped block is broken, its bench hummocky by a quarter of a terrace's drop at half the outermost terrace's width. The outermost blocks scallop the rim crest, out by 0.4 of the outermost terrace's width in each one's middle and in at the cusps between, in place of the crenulation a simple crater keeps; the innermost blocks' toes bulge the floor's edge in by half of the innermost terrace's width. So the wall's width, its terraces and the floor's edge change from block to block around a crater, and none of them is a copy of the outline. The loop draws the plain wall and the scalloped rim; the finish draws the terraces, for the craters it stamps and in a loop crater's restored detail.
- **F68 A porous core dissipates tides.** The rocky interior under an H2O layer is a pile of grains whose porosity falls with the pressure in it as sediments compact: 0.4 at no pressure, falling by a factor e for each 60 MPa of pressure (Athy's law for sandstone, porosity 0.49 falling by e over 3.7 km of burial, Sclater and Christie 1980), each depth's pressure the H2O layer's weight and the interior's own above it; interior.core_porosity is its mean by volume. Above 0.05, where a rock is consolidated, the pores dissipate tides by the friction of grains and the flow of water through them: the eccentricity tides of F13 on the interior's radius with an imaginary Love number 0.84 times the porosity above 0.05, set so that Enceladus's core of 0.23 dissipates 15 GW, within the 10 to 30 GW Choblet et al. (2017) model for a core of 20 to 30 percent porosity. interior.tidal_power adds it to the body's own. So Enceladus's tides come to 30 GW, its heat flux to 38 mW/m², and its shell to 22 km and its elastic lid to 12 km, where they had been 15 GW, 44 km and 23 km: the shell gravity, shape and libration measure is 18 to 25 km (Čadek et al. 2016; Beuthe et al. 2016), and validate now checks it. The large moons' interiors, at gigapascals, are consolidated and dissipate nothing more; a small moon with a porous core and an eccentric orbit is heated from within, as Enceladus is. With the thinner shell, the craters relax more beside its terrains and across its plains, its lid lets lanes open on the lid of the time as well as in its episodes (F61), and its episodes, forty conductive times of the shell apart (F53), still come every 0.52 Gyr.
- **F69 A basin's collapse breaks a thin lithosphere into rings.** A large crater collapses by drawing the weak substrate under the lithosphere into its cavity, and the inflow drags the lithosphere toward the basin until it fails in concentric normal faults (ring tectonics: Melosh and McKinnon 1978; McKinnon and Melosh 1980). The lithosphere through the collapse is the ice colder than 240 K, 0.88 of its melting point (McKinnon and Melosh 1980; Johnson et al. 2013), on the shell's conductive profile at the impact's time and place: Callisto's was 19 km four billion years ago, where McKinnon and Melosh read 15 to 20 km from Valhalla's rings. Melosh's (1982) measure of the drag against the strength, gamma = sqrt(3) S D_tr / (4 Y z), with the drag S = 0.1 rho g H, H half the transient diameter, and the strength of a brittle lithosphere Y z = (1/2)(1 - 1/k) rho g z² for the friction of ice, 0.69 (Beeman et al. 1988), as Singer et al. (2023) evaluate it for Europa's ring graben, comes to 0.06 (D_tr / z)², the transient diameter from McKinnon and Schenk's (1995) scaling. Above 2 the lithosphere fails, and above 5 it breaks into an annulus of graben (Melosh 1982); between, the annulus reaches out by degrees. The drag needs a substrate that flows in: the share of the basin's own wavelength it relaxes within 1 Myr (F39), whole under an ice shell and nothing under a rocky body, whose mantle this model does not relax, so the Moon's basins keep their own rings and no more. The graben run from 3 to 8 transient radii (Tyre 3.6 to 7.7, Callanish 2.8 to 6.9, Valhalla 4.2 to 8.4 with its last ring at 10.2, Asgard 2.5 to 5.9, Ganymede's furrows 1.8 to about 10.4). Each is two normal faults dipping 62 degrees that meet at the base of the lithosphere, so 1.05 times the lithosphere wide (20 km at Valhalla, where they are 15 to 20), the next five widths on (100 km at Valhalla, 75 to 100 measured), spaced wider and narrower outward as the square root of the distance; a graben is as deep as a strain of 2 percent at the annulus's inner edge times its spacing, the strain falling as the square of the distance, its flanks raised by a quarter of its depth and falling away over 0.3 of its width, its faults' footwalls rebounding. Inside the graben, from 1.5 transient radii, the blocks' raised edges stand as ridges 0.6 of the graben's spacing apart, half their width wide and as high as the strain times their spacing (Valhalla's sinuous ridge zone, 55 km apart). Each ring breaks into arcs of 2 to 8 spacings, a quarter of its length in gaps, wandering off it and standing off it en echelon. The arcs are events of the finish at the basin's time, so younger craters overprint them and later lanes renew them, and each keeps the share of its relief its own wavelength keeps to the present (F39).
- **F70 A crater relaxes by wavelength.** An ice shell relaxes each wavelength of a crater's relief on its own timescale (F39): the bowl, its longest, goes first and the floor domes up, while the rim and the wall's terraces, short loads the elastic lid holds, stand (Parmentier and Head 1981; Dombard and McKinnon 2006; Bland et al. 2012 on Enceladus). The radial profile is split into bands between Gaussian blurs a factor of two apart, from the profile table's step to half the crater's radius; each band carries the wavelength at which the difference of its two blurs peaks, 6.54 times the finer blur, and keeps the share of its relief the shell lets that wavelength keep over the crater's life (F39, F66), and the smooth remainder keeps the longest band's share. The terraces, ridge rings and central peak keep the share of a wavelength a quarter of the diameter. The finish relaxes the craters it replays so, and a loop crater's restored detail; until now a replayed crater kept one share of its whole relief, its rim and terraces with its bowl, and a loop crater's restored detail none of its relaxation.
- **F71 A complex crater's relief scales with the strength length.** A complex crater collapses until its slopes stand on the target's strength against its gravity, the length the simple-to-complex transition measures. Its depth, rim height and central peak are those of the lunar crater at the same multiple of the transition (Pike's laws), scaled by the body's transition against the Moon's, and the basin caps scale the same way. So Ganymede's complex craters come out 60 to 70 percent shallower than the Moon's of the same size (Croft 1981): its 40 km craters 0.9 km deep and its 60 km craters 1.0 km, where its central pit craters measure 0.5 to 1.7 km (Bray et al. 2012). Mimas's Herschel, 139 km across, comes out 11 km deep, where it is 10 to 12. The Moon's craters keep Pike's laws, and a body of higher gravity, Mercury, gets complex craters shallower than the Moon's of the same size, as Pike (1988) measured them, where the lunar laws had given every body the Moon's depths.
- **F72 On ice the central uplift collapses into a pit.** A complex crater's central uplift in ice collapses into a central pit, by the drainage of its abundant melt, ice melting under a fraction of the shock pressure rock needs, or by the failure of a weak uplift (Schenk 1993; Alzate and Barlow 2011; Bray et al. 2012), where rock's overshoots and falls outward into a peak ring. From 8.5 transitions a crater on ice has a central pit (Ganymede's peaks give way to pits near 30 km) and from 20 a dome rises in the pit (near 60 km; central dome craters span 60 to 180 km on Ganymede and Callisto); neither has the lunar interior rings or a basin's outer ring, which on ice the lithosphere's own failure makes (F69). The pit is 0.19 of the crater across at its onset, widening as the crater over the onset to the power 0.4, up to 0.4 (the median of 471 pits on Ganymede, Alzate and Barlow 2011; Bray et al.'s 0.2 at 40 km and 0.32 at 100 km); its raised rim spans 0.34 of the diameter as a central peak would, stands as high as a peak would, about 200 m below 50 km, and breaks up around its circuit (Schenk 1993); it is half the crater's depth deep. A dome is 0.6 of its pit across and rises to 0.3 of the crater's depth above the floor (domes up to 1.5 km high in craters 0.8 to 1.6 km deep, Schenk et al. 2004). The pit's outline, its rim and its dome wander each by a shape of its own.
- **F73 Only the rim carries the rim's outline, and the rim's height varies.** A crater's radius was stretched everywhere by its rim's scalloped outline, so its floor's edge, terraces, rings and peak repeated the rim's shape in every contour. Now the stretch is whole at the rim and beyond and falls to nothing across the outer 0.3 of the wall, where the outermost slump blocks that scallop the rim stand (F67), so the inner terraces, the floor and the central features keep shapes of their own: where no terraces shape them, the floor's edge and the bowl take a crenulation of their own as irregular as the rim's, and each ring keeps its own outline, 0.035 of the radius. The rim stands higher or lower around the crater by 0.15 root mean square in low orders (16 to 74 percent around Ganymede's craters, Bray et al. 2012), fading out over its flanks.
- **F74 The plumes' fallout mantles the ground.** Snow laid over billions of years creeps down the slopes of what it covers, filling the hollows and thinning on the crests, so relief smaller than its thickness is buried and what stands larger is rounded by no more than it: Enceladus's old craters are muted (Kirchoff and Schenk 2009). The loose top of the deposit, what drains into fractures, is a few hundred metres (Martin et al. 2023); the whole deposit, sintered below, mantles. The finish draws the ground toward its own low-pass over twice the deposit's thickness since each surface formed (F64), by no more than that thickness either way; the deposit's mean, which would only raise the ground, is left out. The mantle buries only what is older than it: the craters too small to resolve (F54) are laid after it, and only those dug since the cover above them grew thinner than they are deep pit its surface, so a plain under thick fallout keeps the small craters younger than its last few hundred metres of snow.
- **F75 An icy moon's shell dissipates its own tides.** The provisional tidal model gave every body a Love number k₂ and a dissipation factor Q by its composition class, so that an ice shell's tides went as the eccentricity squared whatever the shell's state: Europa's came to 76 mW/m² and its shell to 7.7 km, where Juno's microwave radiometer measures a conductive shell of 29 ± 10 km (Levin et al. 2025), and a pulse of eccentricity would have thinned it without limit.
